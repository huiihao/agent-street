\begin{abstract}
We present a reproducible agent-based market simulation in which sixteen
MBTI-flavoured traders evolve interpretable strategy genomes through reward
learning, imitation, and mutation, while four observer agents publish structured
reports. Using a frozen five-day, five-minute market snapshot and a
four-condition by sixteen-seed ablation (64 runs, 300 ticks each), we find that
reward learning reduces trade synchrony and order-flow imbalance, lowers
cross-sectional return dispersion, increases strategy diversity, and improves
returns and drawdowns relative to fixed priors. Imitation increases coordination: adding it to learning raises synchrony
and worsens drawdown relative to learning alone without a significant return
gain, while imitation alone improves returns relative to static priors but
does not improve drawdown or strategy diversity. The population's guild
composition shifts from a momentum/contrarian split toward a broader ecology
that includes mean-reversion and sentiment strategies.
We then audit the observers. The original composite cascade-risk score is not
predictive of future synchrony, imbalance, or drawdown, and its polarization
term points in the opposite direction from its risk label: polarization behaves
as a disagreement buffer. A revised, order-flow-based observer score predicts
future synchrony (Spearman $\rho=0.75$), absolute order imbalance
($\rho=0.77$), and future drawdown magnitude ($\rho=0.61$) at the run level.
The snapshot is validated against the source endpoint with exact bar-level
agreement, 21 unit tests pass, and all 64 runs satisfy the wealth invariant.
We discuss the tension between individual learning and social imitation, the
need to validate observer metrics component-wise, and the limits of a
single-market-regime study.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} agent-based markets; social learning; imitation;
strategy evolution; observer agents; reproducibility.

\section{Introduction}

\citet{park2023generative} showed that a small town of language-model agents can
produce believable social behaviour. A natural next question is what happens
when those agents are placed in a market: do they learn from their own
profit-and-loss record, do they copy each other's strategies, and can observer
agents tell us anything quantitatively useful about the resulting collective
behaviour? This paper studies those questions in a controlled, reproducible
environment.

Agent-based computational finance has a long tradition of heterogeneous,
adaptive traders \citep{lebaron2006agent,hommes2006heterogeneous} and of
agent-based models as a complement to equilibrium reasoning
\citep{farmer2009economy,tesfatsion2006agent}. Herding and informational
cascades are central mechanisms in that literature
\citep{kirman1993ants,bikhchandani1992theory,cont2000herd}. Our contribution is
not a new asset-pricing model. It is a deliberately inspectable testbed that
combines (i) a validated real-market replay, (ii) evolvable strategy genomes
with explicit accounting, and (iii) four observer agents whose structured
reports can be evaluated against future outcomes.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We present Agent Street, a reproducible market simulation with sixteen
  evolving traders, an explicit House market maker, strict accounting, and four
  observer agents. The code, frozen data snapshot, experiment runner, analysis
  scripts, and figures are public \citep{agentstreet2026}.
  \item In a four-condition ablation on a frozen five-day real-market replay,
  we show that individual reward learning is a \emph{diversifying} force: it
  reduces trade synchrony and order-flow imbalance, increases strategy
  diversity, and improves returns and drawdowns relative to fixed MBTI priors.
  \item Social imitation is a \emph{coordinating} force: adding it to learning
  increases synchrony and order-flow imbalance, does not significantly improve
  returns, and worsens drawdown relative to learning alone. Imitation alone
  improves returns relative to static priors but not drawdown or diversity.
  The combination achieves the best aggregate return but not the lowest
  coordination or drawdown.
  \item We audit the observer agents and show that a plausible composite
  cascade-risk narrative can fail. Its polarization component is associated
  with \emph{lower} future synchronous drawdowns. A revised order-flow-based
  score is strongly predictive of future synchrony, imbalance, and drawdown at
  the run level. The revised score is post-hoc and is presented as a
  hypothesis, not a validated early-warning system.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Agent-based markets.}
Heterogeneous-agent models explain excess volatility, clustering, and market
regimes through interacting beliefs and strategies
\citep{hommes2006heterogeneous,lebaron2006agent}. \citet{kirman1993ants} and
\citet{bikhchandani1992theory} provide canonical mechanisms for recruitment and
informational cascades; \citet{cont2000herd} connects herding to aggregate
fluctuations. Our agents operate in a replay whose price path is exogenous (the
House absorbs residual order flow), which isolates behavioural coordination
from endogenous price impact.

\paragraph{Learning and social transmission.}
Evolutionary and reinforcement perspectives on strategy change go back to
\citet{holland1975adaptation}; learning in games is treated by
\citet{fudenberg1998theory}, and bounded, inductive reasoning by
\citet{arthur1994inductive}. Cultural transmission and social learning are
studied by \citet{boyd1985culture}, and the econometrics of social interactions
by \citet{manski2000economic}. Our genome update is a lightweight
reward-modulated Hebbian rule, not a claim of optimal learning; imitation is
gated by a trust graph and a fitness comparison.

\paragraph{Generative agents and observers.}
The memory/reflection/conversation loop is inspired by
\citet{park2023generative}. We add an explicit market, a strategy genome, an
accounting ledger, and observers whose scalar outputs can be validated. The
broader adaptive-markets view \citep{lo2004adaptive} motivates treating
strategies as changing populations rather than fixed types.

\section{Agent Street}

\subsection{Market replay and reliability}

The market layer loads a single, validated, time-aligned bar timeline. All
symbols share one timestamp index; non-finite prices, duplicate timestamps,
contradictory OHLC values, and obvious bad prints are removed. Bars are cached
and can be loaded from a frozen snapshot for experiments. During a run no
network access is used.

The experimental snapshot contains 390 aligned five-minute bars for AAPL,
GOOGL, MSFT, TSLA, and AMZN over five sessions in October 2026
(2026-10-13 to 2026-10-19, UTC dates). Its SHA-256 is
\texttt{3c9cf0ff864b68e6886c1f3181f67e6}\allowbreak
\texttt{e93dbacef8102007c34fedc768c9f86fe}.
Every symbol has 390 bars, the timestamps are strictly monotonic, and there are
zero duplicate timestamps, non-positive prices, OHLC inconsistencies, or
negative volumes. A re-fetch from the Yahoo chart endpoint for the same window
matched all 390 overlapping closes exactly (maximum relative difference 0.0);
the endpoint returned one additional post-snapshot bar. This verifies snapshot
integrity, not independence of the upstream vendor.

\subsection{Traders and strategy genomes}

Sixteen MBTI-flavoured traders start with a genome containing six signal
weights (trend, reversion, RSI, herding, influence, memory; normalized to sum
to one) and four traits (risk, patience, contrarian, noise). A decision combines
the shared indicators:
\begin{align}
  \text{trend} &= \tanh\!\left(\frac{\text{momentum}_5}{\text{vol}_{20}\sqrt{5}}\right),
  \qquad
  \text{reversion} = -\tanh\!\left(\frac{\text{close}-\text{SMA}_{20}}{\text{ATR}_{14}}\right),\\
  \text{tech} &= w_{\text{trend}}\text{trend}' + w_{\text{rev}}\text{reversion}' + w_{\text{rsi}}\text{rsi},\\
  \text{social} &= w_{\text{herd}}\text{herd} + w_{\text{inf}}\text{influence}
  + w_{\text{mem}}\text{memory},\\
  \text{composite} &= \text{tech}+\text{social}+\epsilon,
\end{align}
where the trend component is flipped by the contrarian gene and the composite
is clipped to $[-1,1]$. Each decision cycle permits one deliberate order per
agent. Risk controls include drawdown panic with cooldown, stop loss, profit
taking, a minimum holding period, position caps, and per-symbol cooldowns.

\paragraph{Experience learning.}
Every buy carries the signal context that produced it. On close, the realized
trade return $r$ becomes a reward
\begin{equation}
  \text{reward}=\operatorname{clip}(r/0.02,-1,1),\qquad
  \Delta w_g=\eta\,\text{reward}\,\text{context}_g,
\end{equation}
with $\eta=0.10$ (1.7$\times$ at the Quant Lab). Risk and patience also adapt.
The update is a lightweight online learner; it is not a claim of optimal
policy learning.

\paragraph{Imitation and mutation.}
After a conversation, a lower-fitness trader may copy a blend of a higher-fitness
playbook, gated by trust:
\begin{equation}
  w_g^{\text{learner}} \leftarrow 0.76\,w_g^{\text{learner}}
  + 0.24\,w_g^{\text{teacher}} + \text{noise}.
\end{equation}
The Quant Lab raises the imitation probability and the Guild Hall raises it
slightly. Every 20 ticks a small fraction of genomes explore a random
variation.

\paragraph{Accounting.}
Orders cross internally with price/time priority and partial fills; residual
orders are absorbed by the House at the real price $\pm$ a half-spread. Fees are
charged per side and capitalized into average cost; sells realize P\&L against
that cost. Every 20 ticks the engine checks
\begin{equation}
  \sum_i \text{cash}_i + \sum_i \text{holdings}_i\cdot p
  + \text{House cash} + \text{House holdings}\cdot p + \text{fees}
  = \text{constant}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Observers}

Four observers read the same state: a Physicist (order parameter, dispersion,
susceptibility), a Mathematician (trust components, partitions, centrality), a
Social Engineer (trust density, polarization, guilds, order flow, cascade
risk), and a Mystic (deliberately unvalidated colour). Reports are generated
every 20 ticks and expose structured metrics alongside prose. The experiment
uses these structured fields, not the prose. Figure~\ref{fig:system} shows the
interactive view; the experiments use the same engine headlessly.

We run 16 seeds per condition (64 runs) for 300 ticks. The replay starts after
30 warm-up bars and does not loop. The market path is identical across all runs;
only agent decisions and interactions differ. LLM enrichment is disabled. The
primary outcomes are aggregate return, max drawdown magnitude, strategy
diversity (one minus the Herfindahl index of guild counts), cross-sectional
return standard deviation, trade synchrony (the larger buy/sell agent count
divided by the number of trading agents), and absolute order imbalance. The
strategy generation and adaptation count serve as a manipulation check.

Statistics use paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests blocked by seed, Holm
correction within the six primary outcomes for each contrast, paired bootstrap
95\% confidence intervals for mean differences, and rank-biserial effect sizes.
Observer predictive tests use run-level Spearman correlations with 5,000
run-cluster bootstrap resamples; report-level correlations are reported as a
secondary view to expose pseudo-replication.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Manipulation and strategy dynamics}

The conditions behave as intended. Mean strategy generation is 0.00 for static
priors, 8.75 for imitation only, 78.25 for learning only, and 75.69 for the full
system; adaptation counts are 0.0, 36.8, 612.6, and 607.8 respectively. The
learning conditions therefore experience many more closed-trade updates than
the imitation-only condition, while the static condition is fixed.

Figure~\ref{fig:primary} shows the distributions; Table~\ref{tab:means} and
Table~\ref{tab:paired} summarize the main result. Relative
to static priors, both learning conditions reduce trade synchrony and order
imbalance, increase strategy diversity, reduce cross-sectional return
dispersion, and improve returns and drawdowns. The full system improves
aggregate return by 0.110 percentage points (Holm $p=.003$), reduces drawdown
magnitude by 0.084 points ($p=.008$), raises diversity by 0.069 ($p=.008$), and
lowers return SD by 0.183 points ($p=.002$). The learning-only condition shows a
similar pattern and the largest drawdown reduction (0.163 points, $p<.001$).

Imitation has a different signature. Adding imitation to learning raises
synchrony by 0.040 ($p<.001$) and absolute imbalance by 0.071 ($p<.001$), does
not significantly improve aggregate return (+0.045, $p=.536$), and worsens
drawdown magnitude by 0.079 points ($p=.021$) relative to learning alone.
Imitation alone raises synchrony (+0.020, $p<.001$) and imbalance (+0.041,
$p<.001$) relative to static priors, improves return (+0.050, $p=.032$) and
reduces return SD (0.141 points, $p=.005$), but does not improve drawdown
($+0.009$, $p=.751$) or diversity ($-0.002$, $p=1.0$). The full system achieves
the best aggregate return in the sample, but not the lowest coordination or
drawdown. The economic magnitudes are small; the statistically robust effects
are behavioural.

\subsection{Observer audit}

The original Social Engineer score combined dominant share, polarization, and
low trust density:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\text{v1}}=0.45\,D + 0.35\,P + 0.20\,(1-\min(1,\rho_{\text{trust}}/0.25)),
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the dominant guild share, $P$ the polarization index, and
$\rho_{\text{trust}}$ the trust density. The revised score uses order-flow
alignment and treats polarization as a buffer:
\begin{equation}
  R_{\text{v2}}=0.55\,S + 0.25\,\min(1,I/3) + 0.20\,D - 0.15\,P,
\end{equation}
where $S$ is trade synchrony and $I$ the number of recent imitation events.

Table~\ref{tab:observer} and Figure~\ref{fig:observer} summarize the audit. The
audit is the most cautionary result. The v1 composite is not predictive of
future synchrony ($\rho=0.029$, $p=.82$), imbalance ($\rho=0.044$, $p=.73$), or
drawdown magnitude ($\rho=-0.133$, $p=.30$). Its polarization term is
\emph{negatively} associated with future synchronous drawdowns
($\rho=-0.546$, $p=3.0\times10^{-6}$), the opposite of its risk interpretation:
a split town is less likely to suffer a correlated drawdown. At the report
level, the v1 composite is negatively associated with future drawdown
magnitude ($\rho=-0.170$, $p=1.2\times10^{-7}$), confirming the sign reversal.

The v2 flow-risk score is positively associated with future synchrony
($\rho=0.754$, $p=6.5\times10^{-13}$), absolute order imbalance
($\rho=0.769$, $p=1.1\times10^{-13}$), and drawdown magnitude
($\rho=0.609$, $p=9.5\times10^{-8}$) at the run level. The report-level
correlation with drawdown is weaker ($\rho=0.095$, $p=.003$), as expected when
much of the between-run variance is averaged away. The flow-risk score is
post-hoc: it was designed after observing the v1 failure on the same runs.
It should therefore be treated as a hypothesis and tested on independent
windows before being used as an early-warning system.

\subsection{Reliability}

All 64 runs satisfy the wealth invariant exactly, all genomes normalize to one,
all sampled trust edges are symmetric, all conversation participants are valid,
all trades have positive shares and prices, and all recorded metrics are finite.
Changing the observer logic from v1 to v2 left every agent-level metric
bit-identical, confirming that observers do not feed back into trading. The
snapshot cross-check is exact for all 390 overlapping closes. The 21 unit tests
cover the data, indicator, matching, accounting, and strategy layers.

\section{Discussion}

The central tension is between individual learning and social imitation.
Learning gives agents different experiences and moves their genomes apart: it
raises strategy diversity, lowers trade synchrony, and reduces cross-sectional
return dispersion. Imitation pulls them together: it raises synchrony and
order-flow imbalance and does not significantly improve returns in the full
system. The full system has the best aggregate return in the sample but a
larger drawdown than learning alone. A system designer who optimizes only
return may choose the full mechanism; one who cares about correlated downside
risk may prefer learning-only or a lower imitation rate.

The observer audit has a broader methodological implication. The original
composite was internally coherent and produced plausible prose, yet it failed
out of sample in the forward direction. Its polarization component had the
wrong sign. This is exactly the kind of failure that narrative reports can hide:
the prose sounds like risk analysis, but the scalar does not track the outcome.
Observers should be evaluated component-wise, against future outcomes, at the
run level, with multiple seeds. The revised score is a useful hypothesis, but it
is not yet a validated warning system.

The economic magnitudes are small. The window is a single declining market, and
the House pins prices to the replay; agents cannot move the market. The results
are about behaviour and coordination, not profitability or alpha. A real
deployment would need multiple regimes, market impact, shorting, and an
independent intraday data source.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single window and regime.} Five sessions of five US large caps in
  October 2026, in a declining market. The effects may not generalize.
  \item \textbf{Exogenous prices.} The House absorbs residual order flow so the
  town price equals the replay price. This isolates behaviour but removes
  endogenous price impact.
  \item \textbf{Asset and trading scope.} Long-only, five symbols, 5-minute bars,
  fixed fees and spread; no shorting, options, borrow costs, or volume limits.
  \item \textbf{Post-hoc observer revision.} The v2 score was motivated by the
  v1 audit on the same runs. Its strong run-level correlation is promising but
  not a pre-registered independent validation.
  \item \textbf{Data independence.} The snapshot is yfinance; the cross-check
  uses the same Yahoo upstream. No independent intraday vendor was available.
  \item \textbf{Rule-based agents and observers.} LLM enrichment was disabled;
  LLM-powered agents may show different dynamics and observer behaviour.
  \item \textbf{No financial claim.} This is a simulation, not investment
  advice, and the returns are not an alpha claim.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

In a controlled replay of real market bars, individual reward learning made a
population of evolving traders more diverse and less coordinated and improved
downside outcomes relative to fixed MBTI priors. Adding social imitation to
learning increased coordination and did not significantly improve returns; the
combination produced the best aggregate return but a worse drawdown than
learning alone. Imitation alone improved returns relative to static priors but
not drawdown or diversity.
The observer audit showed that a plausible composite risk narrative can fail,
while an order-flow-based signal predicts future synchrony, imbalance, and
drawdown. Agent-based market simulations are most useful as behavioural
laboratories when they report coordination and diversity explicitly, validate
observer scalars against outcomes, and avoid mistaking narrative coherence for
predictive validity.
